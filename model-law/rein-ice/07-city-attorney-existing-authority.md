# City Attorney Actions Under Existing Authority

What a city attorney can do **now** — without passing new legislation — when federal immigration agents use excessive force against community members, legal observers, or rapid responders.

This memo focuses on the legal tools available in the **9th Circuit** (covering Arizona, California, Oregon, Washington, and others). Tucson-specific considerations are noted where relevant.

---

## Immediate Action Checklist

After an incident of federal agent excessive force (e.g., pepper spray deployed against non-threatening observers):

**Within 24-48 hours:**

1. **Preserve evidence.** Direct city staff to collect and preserve all available video (body cameras, bystander footage, surveillance), declarations from witnesses and victims, and medical records. Secure physical evidence (clothing, masks affected by chemical agents). Photograph injuries. Identify all officers present if possible.

2. **Contact the state Attorney General's office.** Determine whether the AG is willing to join or support litigation. Joint city-state actions strengthen standing and resource capacity. Precedent: Minnesota AG + Minneapolis/St. Paul joint action (Jan. 2026).

3. **Assess standing.** Identify the city's concrete injury: harm to city residents, chilling effect on civic participation, interference with city-funded programs (rapid response networks, community policing), diversion of city emergency medical resources, harm to public safety cooperation with immigrant communities.

4. **Draft a TRO motion.** If the conduct is ongoing or likely to recur, prepare an application for a temporary restraining order in the U.S. District Court for the District of Arizona. The standard: (1) likelihood of success on the merits; (2) likelihood of irreparable harm absent relief; (3) balance of equities; (4) public interest. *Winter v. NRDC*, 555 U.S. 7 (2008).

5. **Obtain Mayor and Council authorization.** Under the Tucson City Charter, Article III, Section 4, the City Attorney requires direction from the Mayor and Council to initiate litigation. Prepare a briefing for an emergency session if necessary.

6. **File administrative complaints.** Submit complaints to the DHS Office of Inspector General and the DHS Office for Civil Rights and Civil Liberties. These create a formal record regardless of whether litigation proceeds.

7. **Issue a public statement of legal position.** The City Attorney can issue a formal opinion or public statement clarifying the legal rights of community members to observe and record federal law enforcement activity. This costs nothing and establishes the city's position.

## Part I: Legal Theories Available

### A. Injunctive Relief (TRO / Preliminary Injunction)

**What it does:** Court order directing federal agents to stop specific conduct (e.g., deploying chemical agents against non-threatening observers, retaliating against people recording).

**Where to file:** U.S. District Court for the District of Arizona (Tucson Division).

**Standard:** The movant must show all four *Winter* factors. For First Amendment claims, courts apply a "sliding scale" — a stronger showing of likely success on the merits can offset a lesser showing on the other factors. The 9th Circuit recognizes that "the loss of First Amendment freedoms, for even minimal periods of time, unquestionably constitutes irreparable injury." *Elrod v. Burns*, 427 U.S. 347, 373 (1976).

**Precedent:**
- *Tincher v. Noem* (D. Minn. Jan. 2026) — Preliminary injunction granted prohibiting ICE from pepper spraying, arresting, or retaliating against peaceful observers during immigration enforcement. Subsequently stayed by the 8th Circuit on appeal.
- *Dickinson v. Trump*, No. 3:25-cv-02170 (D. Or.) — TRO (Feb. 2026) and preliminary injunction with class certification (Mar. 2026) barring DHS from using chemical or projectile munitions absent imminent threat to officer safety. Currently on appeal to the 9th Circuit (argued Apr. 7, 2026).
- *Index Newspapers LLC v. City of Portland*, 977 F.3d 817 (9th Cir. 2020) — 9th Circuit addressed injunctive relief for press and legal observers at federal enforcement actions.

**Honest assessment:** Injunctions can be obtained at the district court level. The federal government will appeal. The 8th Circuit stayed Minnesota's injunction; the 9th Circuit is more favorable but the outcome of the *Dickinson* appeal (pending) will be determinative. Even a stayed injunction creates a legal record, public attention, and negotiating leverage.

### B. Federal Tort Claims Act (FTCA)

**What it does:** Allows tort claims (assault, battery, false arrest, false imprisonment) against the United States for acts of federal law enforcement officers.

**Statutory basis:** 28 U.S.C. Section 2680(h) (the "law enforcement proviso" — waives sovereign immunity for intentional torts by investigative or law enforcement officers).

**Procedure:**
1. File an administrative claim with the relevant federal agency (DHS/ICE) — **this is a mandatory prerequisite**.
2. The agency has six months to respond.
3. If denied or no response, file suit in federal district court.
4. No jury trial. No punitive damages. Claims against the United States only, not individual agents.

**Why this matters:** After *Egbert v. Boule*, 596 U.S. ___ (2022), Bivens actions (suing individual federal officers for constitutional violations) are largely unavailable in the immigration enforcement context. The FTCA is the surviving path for tort-type recovery against federal law enforcement.

**Limitation:** The city itself may not have FTCA standing — this is primarily a tool for individual victims. The City Attorney's role may be to advise and coordinate, or to support victims' private counsel.

### C. Constitutional Claims (First and Fourth Amendment)

**First Amendment — Right to Record:**

The 9th Circuit recognizes a First Amendment right to record law enforcement officers performing official duties in public. Key authorities:

- *Fordyce v. City of Seattle*, 55 F.3d 436 (9th Cir. 1995) — reversed summary judgment where officer smashed videographer's camera during protest.
- *Askins v. U.S. Dep't of Homeland Security*, 899 F.3d 1035 (9th Cir. 2018) — held there is a First Amendment right to record law enforcement in public; struck down CBP ban on photography at ports of entry.

Pepper spraying an observer who is recording and not interfering is retaliation for protected First Amendment activity. This is well-established in the 9th Circuit.

**Fourth Amendment — Excessive Force:**

Pepper spray deployed at close range against a non-threatening person who is not resisting is excessive force under *Graham v. Connor*, 490 U.S. 386 (1989). The 9th Circuit has a deep body of law on chemical agents:

- *Headwaters Forest Defense v. County of Humboldt*, 240 F.3d 1185 (9th Cir. 2001) — pepper spray applied to eyes/faces of nonviolent protesters; held clearly established excessive force.
- *LaLonde v. County of Riverside*, 204 F.3d 947 (9th Cir. 2000) — once a person is subdued, continued pepper spray exposure is clearly established excessive force.
- *Nelson v. City of Davis*, 685 F.3d 867 (9th Cir. 2012) — pepperball projectiles at compliant individuals without warning; excessive force, qualified immunity denied.
- *Young v. County of Los Angeles*, 655 F.3d 1156 (9th Cir. 2011) — pepper spray on non-threatening person during minor stop; excessive force, qualified immunity denied.
- *Deorle v. Rutherford*, 272 F.3d 1272 (9th Cir. 2001) — force against unarmed, non-threatening individual; conduct "so patently violative" that no closely analogous case was required.

**The Graham factors applied to the Tucson incident pattern:**
1. *Severity of crime:* Observers committed no crime. Even if "obstructing" were alleged, it is at most a minor infraction.
2. *Immediate threat:* Observers holding phones and whistles pose no physical threat.
3. *Active resistance:* Standing nearby and recording is not resistance.

Under 9th Circuit law, this analysis strongly favors finding excessive force.

**Qualified immunity caveat:** These cases involve Section 1983 claims against state/local officers. For federal officers, the threshold question is whether a Bivens claim is available at all (see Section B above). The excessive force standards still inform the FTCA analysis and the merits of any injunctive relief claim.

### D. Coordination with State Attorney General

The Arizona AG may or may not be a willing partner depending on political alignment. If available, joint litigation provides:

- Stronger standing (state's parens patriae authority — municipalities lack this)
- Greater litigation resources
- State-law claims the city cannot bring alone
- Political cover for both parties

Precedent: Minnesota AG Keith Ellison + Minneapolis + St. Paul filed jointly against DHS in January 2026, asserting First and Fourth Amendment violations, state sovereignty, and APA claims.

If the AG is unwilling, the city can still proceed independently under its charter authority, but standing arguments are narrower.

## Part II: Arizona-Specific Considerations

### Charter City Authority

Tucson is a charter city under Arizona Constitution, Article 13, Section 2. The Arizona Supreme Court has held that charter cities are "nearly independent of state legislation" on matters of local municipal concern. *City of Tucson v. State*, 229 Ariz. 172, 273 P.3d 624 (2012).

A lawsuit challenging federal officer conduct that injures city residents and chills civic participation is an exercise of local governmental authority, not an ordinance or regulation that could trigger SB 1487 review.

### SB 1487 Preemption Risk

A.R.S. Section 41-194.01 allows the AG to investigate and sanction local governments whose ordinances or actions violate state law. This is a real constraint on adopting new sanctuary-type ordinances in Arizona.

However, filing a federal lawsuit to protect residents' constitutional rights is not an "ordinance, regulation, order, or other official action" that violates state law. The risk is low for litigation itself. The risk increases if the city simultaneously adopts formal non-cooperation policies.

### SB 1070 Legacy

*Arizona v. United States*, 567 U.S. 387 (2012) struck down most of SB 1070 but upheld Section 2(B) (the "show me your papers" provision). This means Arizona law enforcement has an affirmative obligation to check immigration status during lawful stops when there is reasonable suspicion. This constrains what Tucson police can refuse to do — but it does not affect the city's ability to challenge federal agent conduct against civilians.

### No State Civil Rights Statute

Arizona lacks an equivalent to California's Bane Act (Cal. Civ. Code Section 52.1) or similar state civil rights statutes that create causes of action against government officers for rights violations. This means state tort law (assault, battery) routed through the FTCA is the primary damages path, supplemented by whatever remains of Bivens.

## Part III: Escalation Scenarios

### A. Increased Violence During Enforcement Operations

If federal agents escalate beyond pepper spray to physical assaults, rubber bullets, tasers, or firearms:

**Additional legal tools:**
- The FTCA law enforcement proviso (28 U.S.C. Section 2680(h)) covers assault, battery, false imprisonment, false arrest, and abuse of process by federal law enforcement officers. More severe force strengthens these claims.
- Emergency TRO motions become more urgent and more likely to succeed — the irreparable harm showing is stronger.
- Criminal referrals: The City Attorney can refer conduct to the U.S. Attorney's office (18 U.S.C. Section 242 — deprivation of rights under color of law) or the FBI Civil Rights Division. Realistically, federal self-policing is unlikely under the current administration, but the referral creates a record.
- State criminal referrals: If federal agents commit acts that constitute state-law crimes (assault, aggravated assault under A.R.S. Section 13-1204), the question of whether state prosecutors can charge federal officers acting under color of federal authority is complex. The Supremacy Clause generally prevents state prosecution of federal officers for acts within their authority, but acts outside the scope of lawful authority may be prosecutable. *In re Neagle*, 135 U.S. 1 (1890); *Clifton v. Cox*, 549 F.2d 722 (9th Cir. 1977).

**Evidence priorities with escalation:**
- Medical documentation becomes critical — ER records, photographs of injuries, medical expert declarations on the effects of weapons used.
- Officer identification — badge numbers, vehicle plates, agency identification. Under DHS policy, agents are required to identify themselves. Failure to identify supports claims of operating outside policy.
- Chain of command — identify the supervising officers who authorized or directed the use of force.

### B. Federal Enforcement Activity Near Polling Places or During Elections

If ICE conducts enforcement operations near polling places, at voter registration events, or in ways that intimidate voters:

**Federal statutes:**
- **18 U.S.C. Section 594** — Criminalizes intimidation, threats, or coercion interfering with the right to vote in federal elections. Up to one year imprisonment.
- **52 U.S.C. Section 10307(b)** (Voting Rights Act, Section 11(b)) — Prohibits intimidation, threats, or coercion for voting or attempting to vote. **No intent requirement** — the standard is whether the conduct has the effect of intimidating voters.
- **52 U.S.C. Section 20511** — NVRA criminal penalties for intimidation of persons registering to vote.

**Arizona statutes:**
- **A.R.S. Section 16-1013** — Coercion or intimidation of elector. Covers force, violence, restraint, threats, or intimidation to interfere with free exercise of the elective franchise. Class 1 misdemeanor.
- **A.R.S. Section 16-1004** — Interference with election officer. Class 5 felony.

**What the City Attorney can do:**
1. **Seek a pre-election injunction** in federal court prohibiting federal enforcement operations within a specified radius of polling places during voting periods. The legal theory: federal enforcement activity near polling places, particularly in predominantly immigrant neighborhoods, has the effect of intimidating eligible voters in violation of the VRA and 18 U.S.C. Section 594, regardless of the agents' stated purpose.
2. **Coordinate with the Pima County Recorder and Elections Department** to document any federal enforcement activity near polling locations.
3. **Establish a rapid-reporting protocol** between election observers, the City Attorney's office, and designated pro bono counsel to enable same-day emergency filings.
4. **Request a DOJ election monitoring presence** — historically, the DOJ Civil Rights Division has deployed monitors to jurisdictions with histories of voter intimidation. Under the current administration, this is unlikely to be granted, but the request creates a record.
5. **Coordinate with private election protection organizations** — Election Protection (866-OUR-VOTE), ACLU voter protection programs, and local legal aid organizations can supplement the city's capacity.

**The strongest legal argument:** Enforcement operations in the vicinity of polling places during active voting, in neighborhoods with large immigrant populations, have a demonstrable chilling effect on voter turnout among eligible citizens — including naturalized citizens and U.S.-born members of mixed-status families. This satisfies the VRA Section 11(b) "effect" standard without needing to prove intent to suppress votes.

### C. Federal Agents Interfering with City Government Operations

If federal agents disrupt city council meetings, interfere with city employees performing official duties, or target city-funded programs:

- The city has direct standing based on injury to its governmental functions.
- Tenth Amendment anti-commandeering arguments apply: the federal government cannot direct local government operations. *Printz v. United States*, 521 U.S. 898 (1997); *Murphy v. NCAA*, 584 U.S. 453 (2018).
- Interference with city-funded community programs (rapid response networks, community health workers, etc.) constitutes injury to the city's proprietary interests and strengthens standing.

## Part IV: Practical Considerations

### Standing

A municipality's standing to sue the federal government requires a concrete, particularized injury. The strongest bases for Tucson:

- **Financial injury:** City emergency medical services responding to pepper spray victims. City police resources diverted to manage federal enforcement scenes.
- **Programmatic injury:** Federal conduct undermines city-funded community safety and public health programs that depend on trust between city government and immigrant communities.
- **Democratic participation injury:** Chilling effect on civic participation by community members who fear encountering federal agents at public events, city meetings, or polling places.
- **Employee injury:** If any city employees (including volunteer rapid responders working with city-supported programs) were directly affected.

### Litigation Costs and Resources

Federal litigation against the United States is expensive. Options to manage cost:

- **Pro bono partnerships:** ACLU of Arizona, National Immigration Law Center, and other civil rights organizations may co-counsel or take the lead.
- **Class action:** If multiple individuals were affected, class certification allows shared litigation resources and broader relief. Precedent: *Dickinson v. Trump* (Portland) achieved class certification.
- **Amicus support:** Even if the city does not lead the litigation, it can file amicus briefs in support of private plaintiffs' cases, at minimal cost.

### What an Injunction Actually Achieves

Even if an injunction is stayed on appeal, the litigation:
- Creates a formal judicial record of federal misconduct.
- Generates discovery — compels production of federal agency policies, training materials, and incident reports.
- Provides political and media leverage.
- Deters future misconduct by creating personal accountability risk for supervising officers.
- Establishes precedent for future actions.

### Coordination Across Jurisdictions

Multiple cities facing similar federal conduct can coordinate or file joint actions. Current active litigation as of April 2026:

| Jurisdiction | Case | Status |
|---|---|---|
| Minnesota (AG + Minneapolis + St. Paul) | AG suit v. DHS | Injunction granted, stayed on appeal (8th Cir.) |
| Portland, OR | *Dickinson v. Trump* | Injunction + class cert granted, appeal argued (9th Cir.) |
| Southern California | ACLU SoCal v. DHS | Injunctive relief sought |
| Minnesota (ACLU) | *Tincher v. Noem* | Class action, ongoing |

A Tucson action would add a new factual record from a different jurisdiction within the 9th Circuit, supporting the weight of evidence across cases.
